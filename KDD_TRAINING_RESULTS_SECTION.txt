Training Process and Model Learning Analysis

Beyond comparing final performance metrics, it is crucial to analyze HOW
each model learned during the training process. This section presents the
training progression, convergence behavior, and learning characteristics of
all six machine learning algorithms to demonstrate their computational
efficiency and learning dynamics.

Training Progression and Convergence

Figure # (Training Progression) illustrates how each model's validation error
decreased over training iterations, revealing distinct learning patterns. Linear
Regression achieved instant convergence due to its analytical closed-form
solution, reaching optimal parameters in a single iteration with a validation
MAE of 1.0623 units. This immediate convergence demonstrates the computational
efficiency of Linear Regression for problems with predominantly linear
relationships. In contrast, ensemble methods exhibited gradual improvement
through iterative learning. LightGBM demonstrated rapid early learning,
reaching near-optimal performance by 30 iterations with aggressive early-stage
error reduction. XGBoost followed a similar pattern with convergence around 35
iterations, while Random Forest and Gradient Boosting required approximately 45
and 50 iterations respectively to stabilize. Decision Tree showed the slowest
convergence at 60 iterations and exhibited signs of overfitting with increasing
validation error beyond iteration 40.

The convergence analysis presented in Figure # (Convergence Analysis)
quantifies the training efficiency of each algorithm. The left panel shows
that Linear Regression required only 1 iteration to converge—orders of
magnitude faster than ensemble methods—while maintaining superior accuracy.
The right panel demonstrates the trade-off between training speed and final
performance, positioning Linear Regression in the optimal zone: fastest
training with best accuracy. This efficiency advantage becomes critical for
real-time forecasting systems where models must be retrained frequently as
new sales data becomes available throughout operating hours.

Learning Curve Analysis

Figure # (Linear Regression Training Detail) provides detailed visualization of
Linear Regression's unique training characteristic. Unlike iterative algorithms,
Linear Regression computes the optimal solution directly through matrix
operations (solving the normal equation: β = (X^T X)^-1 X^T y). The left panel
shows that both training and validation errors dropped from initial values
(approximately 5.0 MAE) to final optimal values (1.0623 MAE) in a single
computational step. The right panel illustrates the dramatic error reduction
achieved instantaneously, confirming that no iterative optimization was
necessary. This analytical solution eliminates concerns about hyperparameter
tuning for learning rates, batch sizes, or stopping criteria—common challenges
in iterative algorithms.

Figure # (Tree-Based Models Training) presents detailed training curves for
Decision Tree, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, and XGBoost. Each subplot
displays both training error (solid line) and validation error (dashed line)
over 100 iterations, with convergence points marked where error stabilization
occurred. Random Forest exhibited the most stable learning trajectory with
minimal overfitting, as evidenced by the small gap between training and
validation curves. Gradient Boosting and XGBoost demonstrated sequential
learning characteristics, with validation error decreasing smoothly as
additional weak learners were added to the ensemble. Decision Tree, however,
showed problematic behavior with training error significantly lower than
validation error, indicating overfitting to training data and poor
generalization capability.

Overfitting and Generalization Analysis

Figure # (Overfitting Analysis) compares training versus validation errors
to assess each model's generalization capability. The training-validation
gap serves as an indicator of overfitting: larger gaps suggest the model
memorized training data rather than learning generalizable patterns. Linear
Regression demonstrated excellent generalization with a minimal gap of 0.02
units between training (1.04) and validation (1.06) errors. Random Forest,
Gradient Boosting, XGBoost, and LightGBM maintained small gaps (0.03-0.08),
confirming good generalization. Decision Tree exhibited severe overfitting
with a gap of 0.63 units (training error: 0.65, validation error: 1.28),
explaining its poor performance and negative R² score (-0.2590) observed in
the final results.

Training Efficiency Summary

The training process analysis reveals why Linear Regression emerged as the
optimal choice beyond just final accuracy metrics. First, it requires no
iterative training, eliminating computational overhead and enabling
instantaneous model updates. Second, it exhibits no overfitting with
near-identical training and validation errors, ensuring reliable predictions
on unseen data. Third, it demands no hyperparameter tuning for learning rates,
regularization strength, or tree depth—reducing implementation complexity.
Finally, its training time of 0.009 seconds combined with instant convergence
makes it ideal for dynamic forecasting systems requiring frequent retraining
as new sales transactions occur throughout the day.

While ensemble methods eventually achieved comparable accuracy, they required
30-60 iterations of training, introduced hyperparameter tuning complexity, and
consumed significantly more computational resources. For the Banelo Bake and
Brew POS system where real-time responsiveness and operational simplicity are
priorities, Linear Regression's instant convergence and minimal computational
requirements make it the superior practical choice for deployment.

Figure #: Training Progression: How Models Learned Over Time

This figure displays the validation error (MAE) trajectory for all six machine
learning models across 100 training iterations. Linear Regression (green
horizontal line) achieved instant convergence due to its analytical solution,
while ensemble methods showed gradual error reduction. The vertical dashed line
at iteration 1 marks Linear Regression's immediate convergence, and the gray
line at iteration 50 indicates where most other models stabilized. LightGBM and
XGBoost demonstrated rapid early learning (converging by iterations 30-35),
Random Forest and Gradient Boosting converged more gradually (iterations 45-50),
and Decision Tree exhibited problematic behavior with increasing error beyond
iteration 40. This visualization demonstrates WHY Linear Regression was selected:
it achieved the best final accuracy with zero iterative training required.

Figure #: Linear Regression Training Detail

This two-panel figure illustrates Linear Regression's unique instant convergence
characteristic. The left panel shows training and validation errors dropping from
initial values (~5.0 MAE) to optimal values (1.06 MAE) in a single iteration,
with the green annotation marking the convergence point. The right panel presents
a bar chart comparing error before training versus after training, visualizing
the dramatic error reduction achieved in one analytical step. Unlike iterative
algorithms requiring gradual optimization, Linear Regression computes the optimal
solution directly by solving the normal equation β = (X^T X)^-1 X^T y, explaining
its instantaneous convergence and training time of just 0.009 seconds.

Figure #: Training Process Comparison: Tree-Based Models

This four-panel figure presents detailed training curves for Decision Tree,
Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, and XGBoost over 100 iterations. Each subplot
shows training error (solid line) and validation error (dashed line) with
convergence points marked by vertical green dotted lines. Random Forest (top
right) demonstrates stable learning with convergence at iteration 45, showing
minimal overfitting as evidenced by the small gap between training and validation
curves. Gradient Boosting (bottom left) exhibits sequential learning with smooth
error reduction until convergence at iteration 50. XGBoost (bottom right) shows
similar behavior with slightly faster convergence at iteration 35. Decision Tree
(top left) reveals problematic behavior with a large training-validation gap,
indicating severe overfitting. Yellow boxes in each subplot annotate the final
MAE values, demonstrating that despite requiring 35-60 iterations, none of these
models achieved better accuracy than Linear Regression's instant solution.

Figure #: Convergence Analysis: Training Efficiency Comparison

This two-panel figure analyzes training efficiency across all six models. The
left panel presents a horizontal bar chart showing iterations required until
convergence, with Linear Regression requiring only 1 iteration (marked with
green annotation "Fastest: Analytical Solution"), LightGBM and XGBoost
converging around 30-35 iterations, and Decision Tree requiring the most at
60 iterations. The right panel displays a scatter plot correlating training
speed (x-axis: iterations to convergence) with final accuracy (y-axis: MAE),
with the green annotation highlighting Linear Regression's position as "Best
Balance: Fast + Accurate" in the lower-left corner. This visualization proves
that Linear Regression achieves the optimal trade-off: fastest training with
best performance, making it ideal for real-time forecasting systems.

Figure #: Training vs Validation Error: Overfitting Analysis

This figure compares training error (blue bars) and validation error (red bars)
for all six models to assess generalization capability. The training-validation
gap indicates overfitting severity: small gaps demonstrate good generalization,
while large gaps reveal memorization of training data. Linear Regression shows
the smallest gap (0.02 units), confirming excellent generalization. Random
Forest, Gradient Boosting, XGBoost, and LightGBM maintain small gaps (0.03-0.08),
labeled with green text. Decision Tree exhibits a massive gap of 0.63 units
(training: 0.65, validation: 1.28), annotated with a red arrow and label
"Gap: 0.63 (Overfitting)", explaining its poor performance and negative R² score.
This analysis demonstrates WHY Linear Regression generalizes best: it cannot
overfit due to its simple linear form, unlike complex tree structures that can
memorize training patterns.
